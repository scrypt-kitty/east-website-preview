Our mission & principles

EAST works for environmental stewardship, transparency, accountability, informed public participation, and responsible and collaborative decision-making. We research the record, review the technical and regulatory details, educate the public, build coalitions, and engage in lawful advocacy on the issues that affect our communities and our environment.

  • Accuracy What we say is grounded in documents, facts, and analysis we clearly label as our own.

What is Project Raspberry?

The short version

A Google-linked hyperscale datacenter proposed for Greenfield in Botetourt County — advanced under a shell entity and shrouded in NDAs.

In a nutshell

“Project Raspberry” is the developers' code name — not the project's public identity — for a large data-center development in Greenfield, Botetourt County. It is associated with Google via the shell entity “Helios.” Public officials were reportedly asked to sign non-disclosure agreements before the community was informed.

  • Location: Greenfield, Botetourt County, VA.
  • Scale: hyperscale (industrial) data center campus.
  • Corporate structure: advanced under a shell entity (“Helios”) tied to Google.
  • Secrecy: NDAs reportedly signed by local officials before public disclosure.

Water & power: what a hyperscale datacenter actually consumes

The short version

These facilities draw enormous amounts of water and electricity — the local questions are where it comes from and who pays.

In a nutshell

Hyperscale data centers can consume millions of gallons of water and hundreds of megawatts of power. For a rural county, the pressure lands on local watersheds, the grid, and ratepayers.

  • Cooling can require large, sustained water withdrawals.
  • Power demand can rival that of a small city.
  • Infrastructure upgrades are often subsidized by the public.

History of the Roanoke Valley

EAST is rooted in a specific place — the Roanoke Valley and the Botetourt County uplands around it. This is a plain-language overview, not a textbook.

First peoples

Long before county lines, the Roanoke Valley was home to and traveled by Indigenous peoples, including Siouan-speaking groups such as the Tutelo and Monacan. Salt licks in the valley — the origin of the name “Big Lick” — drew game and people alike.

Frontier settlement & Botetourt County

European settlement pushed up the Great Wagon Road in the 1700s. Botetourt County was formed in 1770. Greenfield — the tract now at the center of the datacenter fight — was the plantation seat of the prominent Preston family.

The railroad boomtown

In 1882 the village of Big Lick was chosen as a junction for the Norfolk & Western Railway and renamed Roanoke — “the Magic City.” The railroad set the pattern of outside industry reshaping the community that echoes today.

Farms, water & the valley today

Beyond the city, the valley and Botetourt's uplands remain farmland, forest, rivers, and the Blue Ridge — which is exactly why a development that demands enormous water and power is a community question.

Botetourt, Roanoke region deserve real water numbers for Google's Project Raspberry

2026-07-09 · Environmental Accountability & Strategy Team analysis verified

Filed under Water & power

📷 Photograph
The water level is at 14.7 feet below full pond at the boat landing at Carvins Cove Natural Reserve on Thursday, July 9, 2026, in Roanoke. (Photograph by Scott Yates / SWVA DCTA)

The public has repeatedly heard that the project may need up to 8 million gallons of water per day. But public records show that behind the scenes, the planning discussions were larger and more troubling. In July 2024, project-related questions asked how to reach 3.5 million gallons per day for "Day 01" and discussed a dedicated water-main extension, tanks and pumps to achieve 10 to 11 million gallons per day to the site. In April 2026, after WVWA asked whether Google wanted to keep a "Day 1 plus Day N" approach or "skip over Day 1" and focus on constructing Day N from the start, a Google representative responded that the preferred approach was to proceed with the Day N framework and skip Day 1.

(The documents above were acquired through a Freedom of Information Act request.) That does not prove WVWA has agreed to provide 10 or 11 MGD. But it does show the public has not been given the full picture. This is not a minor technical detail. Water capacity affects drought planning, reservoir reliability, infrastructure costs, sewer capacity, ratepayer risk, and whether other communities and future users may be constrained. One engineer noted that 3.5 MGD for the first building would likely require improvements beyond currently planned projects.

If the larger water scenario is no longer being pursued, Google, WVWA and Botetourt County should say so clearly and in writing. If it remains part of the plan, the public deserves to know before grading begins and before infrastructure decisions become irreversible. Residents should not be asked to accept one number in public while larger numbers appear in planning records.

The question is simple: what is the true maximum water demand for Project Raspberry, who will pay for the infrastructure, and what happens during drought? Before this project moves forward, those answers should be public. Not later. Now.
